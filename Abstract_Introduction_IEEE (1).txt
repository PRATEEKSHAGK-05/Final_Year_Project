ABSTRACT

The rapid expansion of satellite-assisted and non-terrestrial communication systems has positioned High Altitude Platform Station (HAPS) networks as a critical component of next-generation connectivity, bridging coverage gaps left by conventional terrestrial infrastructure. However, the same characteristics that make HAPS networks attractive, namely wide-area coverage, limited onboard processing capacity, and reliance on wireless backhaul links, also expose them to significant cybersecurity risks, most notably Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks. DDoS attacks overwhelm network resources by flooding target systems with malicious traffic, and their detection becomes considerably more challenging in resource-constrained aerial network environments where computational overhead must be kept minimal. Existing machine learning based intrusion detection approaches often rely on high-dimensional network traffic features, many of which are redundant or weakly correlated with attack behavior, leading to increased computational cost, slower detection times, and reduced model interpretability. These limitations render conventional detection frameworks poorly suited for deployment in HAPS environments, where timely and resource-efficient decision making is essential. To address these challenges, this paper proposes a DDoS detection framework that integrates the Jaya optimization algorithm for feature selection with an XGBoost classifier for attack detection, further enhanced by SHAP based explainable artificial intelligence. The Jaya algorithm is adopted for its parameter-less and computationally lightweight optimization behavior, enabling efficient identification of the most informative network traffic features from the CIC-DDoS2019 dataset. XGBoost is selected for its proven classification accuracy, execution speed, and robustness on structured tabular cybersecurity data, while SHAP is incorporated to provide transparent, feature-level justification for every detection decision, thereby improving analyst trust in the system. The proposed framework is expected to reduce feature dimensionality, lower computational complexity, and deliver interpretable, reliable DDoS detection suitable for practical deployment in HAPS-based communication infrastructures.

INTRODUCTION

Cyber security has become one of the most pressing concerns of the modern digital era, as the increasing dependence of individuals, organizations, and critical infrastructures on interconnected systems has expanded the attack surface available to malicious actors. Every additional network endpoint, communication link, and connected device introduces a potential entry point for exploitation, and the consequences of a successful attack now extend far beyond isolated data breaches to include service disruption, financial loss, and compromised public safety. This growing threat landscape has made the design of robust, intelligent, and efficient security mechanisms an essential research priority across virtually every domain of networked computing.

The past decade has witnessed an extraordinary growth in internet-enabled systems, driven by the proliferation of mobile devices, cloud computing platforms, and the Internet of Things. Billions of devices now communicate continuously across heterogeneous networks, generating traffic volumes that were unimaginable only a few years ago. Alongside this growth, network architectures themselves have evolved to meet rising connectivity demands, particularly in regions where traditional terrestrial infrastructure is impractical or economically unfeasible to deploy. High Altitude Platform Station networks have emerged as a promising solution to this challenge. Operating in the stratosphere at altitudes typically between seventeen and twenty-two kilometers, HAPS systems combine the wide coverage advantages of satellite communication with the lower latency and easier maintainability associated with terrestrial networks. Their ability to provide connectivity to remote, rural, and disaster-affected regions, as well as to supplement existing cellular and satellite networks, has made HAPS a subject of significant interest for next-generation communication infrastructure, including its role in supporting fifth and sixth generation network architectures.

Despite these advantages, the security of HAPS networks remains an underexplored area, and their unique operational characteristics introduce distinct vulnerabilities. Among the most disruptive threats facing any networked communication system is the Distributed Denial of Service attack, in which an adversary overwhelms a target system or network link with a large volume of malicious traffic originating from multiple sources, with the intent of exhausting available bandwidth, processing capacity, or connection resources. The result is a degradation or complete denial of service to legitimate users. In the context of HAPS networks, where onboard processing resources are inherently constrained and backhaul links may already operate near capacity, the impact of a DDoS attack can be considerably more severe than in conventional terrestrial networks, potentially disrupting connectivity for entire regions dependent on a single platform.

Detecting DDoS attacks reliably is a persistently difficult problem for several reasons. Attack traffic is often designed to closely resemble legitimate network behavior, making simple threshold based or signature based detection methods increasingly ineffective against evolving attack strategies. Attackers frequently employ distributed and spoofed traffic sources, complicating efforts to trace and isolate malicious flows in real time. Furthermore, the sheer diversity of DDoS attack types, ranging from volumetric floods to protocol level exploitation at the network and transport layers, means that a detection system must generalize well across multiple attack patterns rather than relying on attack-specific heuristics.

To address these challenges, researchers have increasingly turned to machine learning based approaches for DDoS detection, leveraging the ability of learning algorithms to identify subtle statistical patterns in network traffic that distinguish malicious flows from benign ones. Classifiers trained on labeled network traffic datasets, such as flow-based intrusion detection datasets that capture packet-level and flow-level statistics, have demonstrated considerable success in distinguishing attack traffic across a range of scenarios. However, despite these advances, existing machine learning based detection methods continue to face several practical limitations that hinder their real-world adoption, particularly in resource-constrained environments such as HAPS networks.

A primary limitation lies in the high dimensionality of network traffic data. Modern flow-based datasets often capture upwards of seventy to eighty statistical features per traffic flow, encompassing packet timing, size distributions, protocol flags, and connection state information. While this rich feature space can capture nuanced attack signatures, it also introduces substantial computational complexity during both training and inference, a burden that is particularly problematic for HAPS platforms where onboard processing capability is limited by power and payload constraints. Compounding this issue, many of these features are redundant or only weakly correlated with actual attack behavior, meaning that a significant portion of the computational effort expended during detection contributes little to overall accuracy. Beyond computational concerns, another critical shortcoming of many existing detection frameworks is their lack of explainability. Most machine learning classifiers, particularly ensemble and deep learning based models, operate as opaque decision-making systems, providing a classification outcome without any insight into which features or traffic characteristics drove that decision. In security-critical applications, this absence of transparency undermines analyst trust and complicates the process of validating, auditing, or refining detection systems, an especially pressing concern for infrastructure supporting HAPS communication where operational reliability is paramount.

Given these challenges, feature selection has emerged as a crucial preprocessing step in the design of efficient intrusion detection systems. By identifying and retaining only the most informative subset of features from a high-dimensional traffic dataset, feature selection techniques can substantially reduce computational overhead while preserving, and in some cases improving, detection accuracy. Among the various optimization-based feature selection techniques available, the Jaya algorithm presents several properties that make it particularly well suited to this task. Unlike many metaheuristic optimization algorithms that require careful tuning of multiple control parameters, the Jaya algorithm follows a comparatively simple optimization strategy and is inherently parameter-less, relying only on the population size and number of iterations rather than algorithm-specific tuning parameters. This simplicity translates directly into faster convergence and reduced implementation complexity, both of which are valuable characteristics when the goal is to select a compact and highly informative subset of network traffic features without introducing additional computational burden. These qualities make the Jaya algorithm a practical candidate for reducing the dimensionality of large-scale DDoS traffic datasets while retaining the features most relevant to distinguishing attack behavior from normal traffic.

Once an optimized feature subset has been obtained, the choice of classification algorithm becomes equally important in determining overall detection performance. XGBoost, a gradient boosting framework built upon ensembles of decision trees, has been selected for this role due to its consistently high classification performance across a wide range of structured, tabular datasets, a data format that closely matches the flow-based statistical features present in network intrusion detection datasets. Beyond its accuracy, XGBoost is recognized for its computational efficiency and fast execution during both training and inference, along with its robustness to noisy or imperfect training data, characteristics that are particularly valuable in cybersecurity applications where datasets may be imbalanced or contain inconsistencies. Its widespread and successful application across numerous intrusion detection and network security studies further supports its suitability for the DDoS detection task addressed in this work.

While accurate classification is essential, understanding why a model arrives at a particular decision is equally important, particularly in security operations where analysts must be able to justify and act upon automated detection outcomes. To this end, this work incorporates SHapley Additive exPlanations, commonly known as SHAP, as its explainable artificial intelligence component. SHAP provides a principled, game-theoretic approach to quantifying the contribution of individual features toward a specific prediction, enabling transparent, feature-level interpretation of the classifier's decision-making process. By revealing which network traffic characteristics most strongly influence the detection of a DDoS attack, SHAP not only improves the interpretability of the proposed system but also strengthens analyst confidence in its outputs, an essential requirement for any intrusion detection system intended for practical deployment.

Building upon these considerations, this paper proposes a DDoS detection framework specifically designed for application within HAPS network environments. The proposed pipeline begins with the collection and preprocessing of network traffic data, followed by feature selection using the Jaya algorithm to identify a reduced yet highly informative subset of traffic features. The selected features are then used to train an XGBoost classifier capable of distinguishing between benign and malicious network traffic. Finally, SHAP is applied to the trained classifier to generate feature-level explanations for its predictions, offering transparency into the decision-making process of the detection system. Through this integrated approach, the proposed framework aims to address the dual challenges of computational efficiency and explainability that limit the practical adoption of existing DDoS detection methods in resource-constrained aerial network environments.

The expected contributions of this research are threefold. First, the work aims to demonstrate the effectiveness of the Jaya algorithm as a lightweight, parameter-less feature selection technique for reducing the dimensionality of large-scale DDoS traffic datasets without compromising the informativeness of the selected features. Second, it seeks to establish that an XGBoost classifier trained on this optimized feature subset can deliver efficient and reliable DDoS detection performance suitable for deployment in resource-constrained HAPS network environments. Third, the integration of SHAP based explainability is intended to provide a transparent and interpretable detection framework, allowing network security analysts to understand and trust the reasoning behind each detection outcome. Collectively, these contributions aim to advance the design of practical, efficient, and explainable intrusion detection systems tailored to the emerging security requirements of HAPS-based communication infrastructure.

The primary objective of this work, therefore, is to design and propose a DDoS detection framework that combines Jaya-based feature selection, XGBoost classification, and SHAP-based explainability to achieve efficient, accurate, and interpretable detection of DDoS attacks within High Altitude Platform Station networks.
